Human coordination fails without architecture. We do not spontaneously
    resolve competing claims on shared resources, verify that work was
    actually performed, or hold parties accountable when promises break. The
    institutions we call firms, contracts, and markets exist because
    coordination under bounded trust is expensive—and someone has to pay the
    overhead. A firm is an authority-allocation device: it replaces a market
    for every task it internalizes, substituting an internal chain of command
    for the negotiation costs of open contracting. A contract is a
    verification and accountability architecture: it specifies what counts as
    performance, who judges it, and what happens when judgment fails. A
    market is a dispute-escalation interface: prices aggregate heterogeneous
    preferences into a single comparable signal, converting political
    conflict into competitive equilibrium.
    Each of these institutions solves a coordination problem that does not
    disappear when the actors change. We are now fielding agents that accept
    mandates, produce artifacts, and hand results back to downstream systems.
    The coordination problems those agents face are structurally identical to
    the ones human institutions were built to solve. The only question is
    whether the solutions emerge by design or by crisis.
    Authority allocation — In a firm, a principal delegates to an agent
    and accepts that the agent's judgment may diverge from the principal's
    preferences within defined bounds. In a hub-and-worker system, the hub
    dispatches a brief specifying the outcome but not the method. The brief
    is a mandate. The worker's authority to choose HOW is bounded by the
    acceptance needle defined in the brief: the task is complete when the
    output clears the needle, not when the worker decides it is done. This is
    not different in kind from a manager approving an employee's scope of
    discretion.
    Verification — Contracts require observable performance. In agent
    systems, verification takes the form of acceptance tests embedded in the
    dispatch protocol. A brief defines what "done" means as a set of
    measurable conditions. The registry of needles passed or failed is the
    agent-era analogue of a countersignature: verification is structural, not
    optional.
    Accountability — When a contracted party fails to perform, the
    contract provides escalation: renegotiation, penalty, or exit. In a
    dispatching hub, accountability is expressed through acceptance rates and
    retry routing. A brief that fails its needle is escalated to a different
    worker, and the failure is recorded against the originating worker's
    registry. Downstream systems consume that registry. Accountability is
    persistent and composable.
    Dispute escalation — Markets resolve conflicting claims through
    price. Agent systems resolve conflicting claims through escalation
    chains: a brief that cannot be satisfied within the defined needle space
    is flagged, rerouted, or decomposed into sub-briefs. The escalation chain
    is the institution. It is not invented; it is discovered in the pattern
    of how systems handle failure.
    We do not choose whether agents get institutions. We choose whether we
    design the coordination layer or allow it to emerge from the accumulated
    accidents of systems that were never designed for each other. The history
    of human institutions is a history of repeated coordination failures
    producing increasingly explicit rules. We are early in that sequence with
    agent systems. The question before us is whether we read the human record
    and design deliberately—or wait for the accidents to accumulate and then
    write the rules in response to the damage.

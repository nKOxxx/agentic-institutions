## IV. IMPLICATIONS FOR AGENTIC MARKET DESIGN

If institutions are cost-optimal control laws for delegated cognition, market design is not a discretionary engineering choice — it is a structural consequence. The dominance condition (★) and its Jevons rebound tell us which markets will exist, which will be large, and which are still missing. We work through each in order.

**Mandate Marketplaces.** A mandate is a bounded authorization to act on behalf of a principal, machine-readable and verifiable before commitment. If mandates are tradeable, a market for delegation rights emerges: principals auction bounded briefs, workers bid with attested capabilities, and the acceptance probability p is estimated from registry records. This is structurally identical to a labor marketplace, except the employment contract is a machine-verifiable mandate with on-chain enforceability. The infrastructure gap is a standard mandate description language — something the IETF or IEEE could specify the way they specified TLS ciphersuite negotiation. [CITE: SoK 2610.01756 — decentralized agent economic infrastructure survey discusses agent marketplace primitives and identifies mandate specification as an open design problem]

**Registry Standards.** Capability registries are the trust substrate for routing. A worker without a verifiable attestation cannot price its services; a hub without a registry cannot distinguish a reliable worker from one optimizing for acceptance needles. Registry standards serve the same function as credit bureaus in human finance: they convert raw reputation into a priced, transferable signal. ERC-8004 provides the on-chain identity primitive [CITE: ERC-8004]; the attestation layer atop it — signed performance records, domain-specific competency tests, historical p estimates — is the missing standard. Several projects are building in this space; none has converged on a schema the way x509 converged for human web identity. [CITE: Governing AI Agents 2501.07913 — reviews agent governance frameworks and notes the registry problem as a prerequisite for accountability]

**Acceptance-Verification as a Paid Service.** The Jevons rebound is the most commercially significant structural implication. When worker cognition approaches zero marginal cost, dispatch volume expands until hub verification bandwidth is the binding constraint. This means verification is not a side effect of institutional design — it is the primary economic relationship in machine-to-machine markets. In our hub-and-worker institution, the hub runs acceptance needles on every returned artifact: structure counts, budget alarms, policy gate checks. These checks are not free. They are the scarce factor. A market for verification-as-a-service follows directly: third-party auditors that accept a brief, a worker artifact, and a mandate, and return a cryptographically signed acceptance verdict. The verifier does for acceptance what a payment processor does for card transactions — provides a trusted intermediary that both parties can rely on without trusting each other.

**Escalation Insurance.** Every mandate hits a boundary. The brief did not anticipate the situation; the capability registry has no attested resolution path; the acceptance needle cannot fire because no needle covers this case. The escalation path is the institution's last-resort mechanism. If escalation is deterministic — retry, slice-read, brief-rewrite, route to a specialist — then the cost of escalation is bounded and insurable. Escalation insurance is a natural market: principals pay a premium against the risk that their agent's mandate is exceeded, and the insurerunderwrites the escalation cost. This is structurally identical to product liability insurance, with the same actuarial logic. The insurer has the same interest as the registry — accurate, verifiable performance data — and creates the same incentive to improve p.

**Open Problems.** Three are urgent. Mandate portability: if an agent holds a mandate from principal A and is invoked by principal B, does the mandate bind? Current protocols say nothing. Cross-organizational escalation: when an agent operating under mandate M in organization O₁ must act in O₂'s domain, the escalation path crosses a trust boundary that neither organization controls. This is the hardest open problem in the stack, and it is not a communication problem — A2A handles communication. It is an institutional problem: who resolves, under what rules, with what enforcement? Machine liability: when an agent acts beyond its mandate, who is liable, and how is liability settled on-chain? No major jurisdiction has resolved this. The practical consequence is that principals must hold unlimited liability for their agents' actions — a structure that will suppress agentic commerce until it changes. [CITE: SoK 2610.01756 on machine liability and legal personhood open problems; CITE: Governing AI Agents 2501.07913 on accountability gaps in current agent governance proposals]

The market design we have described is not speculative. Mandate marketplaces exist in prototype (agent dispatch systems with brief auctions). Registry standards are converging. Verification-as-a-service is already paid for inside our institution, and the pattern is general. Escalation insurance and cross-org escalation are the missing pieces — and they are missing not because the economics are unclear but because the institutional substrate (mandate language, escalation protocol, liability standard) has not yet stabilized. That stabilization is the urgent work.
